# Hybrid deal threshold with market analysis

A deal is a listing priced meaningfully below fair price, but "meaningfully below" cannot be a single fixed number. A 10% drop on a $40 thin client is noise; the same 10% on an $800 GPU is real. Worse, whole product categories experience **price surges** driven by external events — the 2025–26 DRAM shortage, where AI demand starved the consumer market and repriced RAM across the board — during which listings that look cheap against old expectations are actually priced at the new elevated norm.

We decided the deal threshold is a **hybrid**: a simple per-product floor the user sets and reasons about, refined by **market analysis** that observes each product's actual price distribution over time. The floor is a **percentage** below fair price, with a **global default** and **per-product overrides**. Market analysis may **raise** the threshold where a product's observed prices are noisy, but **never lower it below the floor** — the floor is the user's guaranteed minimum bar, and refinement exists to guard against noise, not to flag thin margins as deals.

The refinement reads a **90-day trailing window** of price observations, pooled across all marketplaces and all conditions for that product. Before computing stats, an **IQR fence** drops extreme outlier listings. The center is the **median**, the spread is the **MAD** (median absolute deviation); both are robust to the desperate-seller and delusional-ask listings common in used markets. Below ~30 observations the product is in **cold start**: the floor applies alone and the UI labels it "low data."

**Surges are absorbed, not special-cased.** Because fair price is computed over the recent trailing window, a sustained rise moves fair price with it, and listings that only looked cheap against old memory stop qualifying. The app **surfaces** a detected surge (30-day median up >15% over the prior 30 days, clearing only when it falls back below +5%) so the user understands why nothing qualifies rather than seeing a silent gap.

**Marketplaces are treated as generic.** We do not apply a per-marketplace adjustment. The adjustment from ADR 0002 was justified for pooling *sold* prices, where eBay and Facebook transaction prices genuinely differ. We have no sold data (ADR 0004); we compare a listing's effective price against the *typical observed asking price*, and both sides are ask-based — same scale, no normalization needed. The consequence is deliberate: **fair price means "typical ask," not "what it sells for."** This matches how the user operates (negotiation starts from the ask) and avoids a correction we cannot validate. This supersedes the per-marketplace-adjustment concept of ADR 0002 in full.

**Search-first framing.** The app is a single pane of glass: the user searches a product across marketplaces and compares prices, or sets up watches. The floor makes deal detection work from day one with no data. Backend scheduled processes (daily) accumulate observations and trend analysis as a **progressive enhancement** that sharpens search and enables proactive deal notification over time — never a gate the user waits behind.

Rejected: a single global threshold (too crude across product classes), a purely data-derived threshold (a modeling layer before there is data to model, and opaque to the user), and per-marketplace adjustment (solves a sold-data problem this ask-based design does not have).

Deferred: a cap on how far refinement can raise the threshold, and negotiation-target output for barter marketplaces.
